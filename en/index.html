Before you begin: ages, responsibilities, and evidence
  • This guide is for children and their caregivers. Children gradually practice expression, cooperation, judgment, and responsibility. Adults provide the conditions, examples, protection, and age-appropriate support. Their responsibilities are not equal.
  • The entries for ages 1–18 help you find material; they are not developmental pass marks. Development, school entry, and puberty vary. Body and safety education should continue over time, without waiting for a particular birthday. Adults mainly narrate and practice with children ages 1–6; from age 7, choose shared or independent reading according to ability.
  • Start with the current age and one real problem. Try one change and observe whether it fits your family. Do not turn the whole guide into a checklist of compulsory achievements. Practice periods are scheduling examples, not scientifically established deadlines for improvement.
  • Effort estimates are qualitative, and intended improvements are goals, not guarantees. Priorities are editorial: act urgently for current danger, build the foundations for basic conditions, and use when needed for specific difficulties. Escalation signs in ordinary entries also deserve immediate attention.
  • Each entry separates principles supported by professional sources from the steps, scripts, and exercises written for this guide. This is not a systematic review. It does not assign falsely precise A/B/C evidence grades or turn associations into causal claims. Books help understanding; they do not automatically prove a method works.
  • References are limited to public materials from official and professional organizations in China and developed countries including the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada. Foreign laws, helplines, medical procedures, and school systems are not transplanted here. Check the arrangements where you live.
  • This edition was researched and written with AI assistance and has not received independent review by child-development, education, or clinical experts. It is general educational information, not diagnosis, treatment, or crisis-response training. For current danger, contact local emergency services and appropriate professionals.

A01 · 1 years

Feel secure, then explore

Responsive interaction, shared attention, movement, and daily rhythms

Read more +

What children can practice

  • I can use pointing, looking, and sounds to express needs; an adult can help put them into words.
  • I can handle safe objects, imitate actions, and look at pictures with someone.

What parents can do

  1. Follow what interests your child, use short sentences, and wait for a response instead of continually testing them.
  2. Use dressing, meals, and walks for interaction; put dangerous objects out of reach first.
  3. Keep handovers between caregivers predictable. Adults must provide safety rather than expecting a toddler to remember every rule.

When this happens

Repeatedly throwing a spoon: consider whether your child is finished eating or exploring. Put utensils away safely, show where unwanted items go, and offer something safe to drop. Crying at separation: say a brief goodbye, hand over to a familiar adult, and reconnect warmly on return.

Try this today

Notice one moment when your child points. Look at the same thing, name it, and wait for their response.

What to look for

Look for easier communication and participation, not the number of written words recognized.

What to avoid

Do not label crying as manipulation or expect a toddler to manage every emotion alone.

Time and effort

What children can practice
