## Before you begin: ages, responsibilities, and evidence

- This guide is for children and their caregivers. Children gradually practice expression, cooperation, judgment, and responsibility. Adults provide the conditions, examples, protection, and age-appropriate support. Their responsibilities are not equal.
- The entries for ages 1–18 help you find material; they are not developmental pass marks. Development, school entry, and puberty vary. Body and safety education should continue over time, without waiting for a particular birthday. Adults mainly narrate and practice with children ages 1–6; from age 7, choose shared or independent reading according to ability.
- Start with the current age and one real problem. Try one change and observe whether it fits your family. Do not turn the whole guide into a checklist of compulsory achievements. Practice periods are scheduling examples, not scientifically established deadlines for improvement.
- Effort estimates are qualitative, and intended improvements are goals, not guarantees. Priorities are editorial: act urgently for current danger, build the foundations for basic conditions, and use when needed for specific difficulties. Escalation signs in ordinary entries also deserve immediate attention.
- Each entry separates principles supported by professional sources from the steps, scripts, and exercises written for this guide. This is not a systematic review. It does not assign falsely precise A/B/C evidence grades or turn associations into causal claims. Books help understanding; they do not automatically prove a method works.
- References are limited to public materials from official and professional organizations in China and developed countries including the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada. Foreign laws, helplines, medical procedures, and school systems are not transplanted here. Check the arrangements where you live.
- This edition was researched and written with AI assistance and has not received independent review by child-development, education, or clinical experts. It is general educational information, not diagnosis, treatment, or crisis-response training. For current danger, contact local emergency services and appropriate professionals.

### A01 · 1 years · Feel secure, then explore

Responsive interaction, shared attention, movement, and daily rhythms

**What children can practice**

- I can use pointing, looking, and sounds to express needs; an adult can help put them into words.
- I can handle safe objects, imitate actions, and look at pictures with someone.

**What parents can do**

- Follow what interests your child, use short sentences, and wait for a response instead of continually testing them.
- Use dressing, meals, and walks for interaction; put dangerous objects out of reach first.
- Keep handovers between caregivers predictable. Adults must provide safety rather than expecting a toddler to remember every rule.

**When this happens:** Repeatedly throwing a spoon: consider whether your child is finished eating or exploring. Put utensils away safely, show where unwanted items go, and offer something safe to drop. Crying at separation: say a brief goodbye, hand over to a familiar adult, and reconnect warmly on return.

**Try this today:** Notice one moment when your child points. Look at the same thing, name it, and wait for their response.

**What to look for:** Look for easier communication and participation, not the number of written words recognized.

**What to avoid:** Do not label crying as manipulation or expect a toddler to manage every emotion alone.

### A02 · 2 years · Let me try, while keeping clear limits

Autonomy, short instructions, and naming feelings

**What children can practice**

- I can choose between two safe options.
- I can practice saying “no” and “help”; adults can demonstrate when I cannot.

**What parents can do**

- Give one instruction at a time, with a gesture and time to respond.
- Allow extra time for eating independently or putting away one toy.
- Allow feelings while immediately stopping hitting and dangerous behavior.

**When this happens:** Wanting a toy in a shop: acknowledge the wish and keep the purchasing decision consistent; move somewhere quieter if needed. Biting: separate the children, care for the injured child first, and teach “stop” or “help.” Never bite back.

**Try this today:** Choose one daily task and let your child choose its order or the item to use.

**What to look for:** Notice whether transitions become easier with support; the goal is not to eliminate all crying.

**What to avoid:** Avoid offering a false choice about a necessary safety rule, or repeatedly changing a limit in response to crying.

*Editorial organization: ages are navigation aids, not developmental tests. See the linked entries for supporting guidance.*

### A03 · 3 years · Ask for help and gradually join group life

Starting preschool, self-care, taking turns, and communication

**What children can practice**

- I can say “I need the toilet” or “Please help.”
- My own belongings can have boundaries; shared items require taking turns.

**What parents can do**

- Use pretend play to rehearse preschool routines and asking for help.
- Ask staff what happens after goodbye, rather than judging only the tears at the door.
- Handle toileting accidents calmly and practice according to readiness.

**When this happens:** Refusing preschool: use a consistent handover and investigate distress that continues during the day. Not lending a toy: distinguish personal from shared belongings, and put away treasured items before visitors arrive.

**Try this today:** Act out: “I cannot open my water bottle; I can ask my teacher.”

**What to look for:** Look for growing confidence in seeking a trusted adult and joining activities.

**What to avoid:** Never threaten not to collect your child or force sharing to impress other adults.

*Editorial organization: ages are navigation aids, not developmental tests. See the linked entries for supporting guidance.*

### A04 · 4 years · Express feelings and begin making things right

Cooperation, emotions, and distinguishing events from imagination

**What children can practice**

- I can say “I do not like that.”
- After a mistake, I can help tidy up, return something, or care for someone with adult support.

**What parents can do**

- Talk about characters’ feelings during stories without turning reading into an examination.
- Describe what happened instead of calling your child bad, stupid, or a liar.
- Teach correct names for body parts and how to seek help.

**When this happens:** Denying a spilled cup: do not set a trap; state what you saw and clean up together. Crying after losing a game: allow disappointment, stop throwing pieces, and rehearse ending a game once calm.

**Try this today:** Practice one boundary sentence: “I am still playing. Please wait.”

**What to look for:** Look for another way to communicate or repair a mistake, rather than expecting no mistakes.

**What to avoid:** Do not expose embarrassing incidents publicly or use humiliation to make a lesson memorable.

### A05 · 5 years · Prepare everyday skills for school

Self-care, play rules, communication, and small completed tasks

**What children can practice**

- I can organize my belongings and ask about things I do not understand.
- I can finish one small step and then try the next.

**What parents can do**

- Practice washing hands, toileting, dressing, and tidying with picture prompts if helpful.
- Explore stories, counting, and construction play without turning every activity into an academic lesson.
- Protect free play, movement, and rest; adapt tasks to current ability.

**When this happens:** Leaving a task unfinished: check difficulty and interest, then reduce it to a manageable step. Reluctance to write: reduce repetitive copying and use drawing or cutting activities; investigate persistent difficulties.

**Try this today:** Make a picture card with three steps for leaving home, and let your child point to each completed step.

**What to look for:** Notice communication and familiarity with routines, not how many schoolbooks have been completed early.

**What to avoid:** School readiness is not the same as finishing the first-year curriculum in advance.

*Editorial organization: ages are navigation aids, not developmental tests. See the linked entries for supporting guidance.*

### A06 · 6 years · Settle into school through workable routines

School-based help, daily schedules, supplies, and starting work

**What children can practice**

- I can tell an adult what I do not understand or where I feel unwell.
- I can pack with a checklist and learn how to recover from forgetting something.

**What parents can do**

- Attend to rest and physical needs before arranging homework.
- Teach organization alongside your child, then gradually step back.
- Check homework difficulty and expected duration with the teacher.

**When this happens:** Forgetting supplies: help resolve the immediate problem if necessary, then find the missing checklist step that evening. Crying over homework: distinguish confusion, fatigue, fear of errors, and handwriting demands; address one obstacle first.

**Try this today:** Gather tomorrow’s belongings in one place and check them together tonight.

**What to look for:** Look for clearer prompts and willingness to ask for help.

**What to avoid:** Never withhold essential medicine, food, or safety equipment to “teach a lesson.”

### A07 · 7 years · Learn to begin without constant reminders

Starting tasks, checking work, and contributing at home

**What children can practice**

- I can begin with the first small step.
- I can complete an appropriate household task.

**What parents can do**

- Agree on a starting point and use a visible checklist instead of repeated nagging.
- Practice one habit at a time, such as reading instructions before writing.
- Give specific feedback on strategies, rather than judging intelligence or carelessness.

**When this happens:** Slow homework: identify whether the barrier is starting, understanding, or writing, and change one key factor. Frequent reports about peers: check safety first; rehearse handling ordinary disagreements, but intervene in threats or harm.

**Try this today:** Ask your child to name tonight’s first step, then wait until the agreed check-in.

**What to look for:** Compare with their own recent experience and notice whether starting is easier.

**What to avoid:** Do not interpret every lapse of attention as deliberate, or dismiss all requests for help.

### A08 · 8 years · Take responsibility without fearing honesty

Honesty, ownership, repair, and practical strategies

**What children can practice**

- I can explain what really happened and help put it right.
- I should ask before taking someone else’s belongings.

**What parents can do**

- State facts without trick questions or labels.
- Acknowledge honesty while still addressing unfinished work or damage.
- Practice borrowing, returning, and managing belongings in small situations.

**When this happens:** Hiding unfinished homework: find the obstacle, plan completion, and consider whether adult anger encourages concealment. Taking something without permission: discuss privately, return it, and arrange appropriate repair; investigate repeated incidents.

**Try this today:** Use one small mistake to demonstrate “explain—repair—plan for next time.”

**What to look for:** Notice whether problems are reported sooner, not just whether the number of mistakes falls.

**What to avoid:** Do not promise there will never be consequences for telling the truth, or punish more harshly after an admission.

### A09 · 9 years · Make friends while keeping personal boundaries

Friendship, self-evaluation, and resolving disagreements

**What children can practice**

- I can invite someone to join me and accept that they may decline.
- I do not have to face repeated targeting alone.

**What parents can do**

- Listen to what happened and distinguish a disagreement from ongoing harm.
- Offer different opportunities for connection rather than relying on one group.
- Do not judge a friend’s worth by family income or grades.

**When this happens:** “Nobody likes me”: ask about specific situations and exceptions, then identify possible connections. Copying insults: explain their impact and practice alternatives; hurting others to fit in still requires intervention.

**Try this today:** Practice an invitation: “Would you like to do this with me?”

**What to look for:** Notice whether your child has someone to turn to and can express boundaries.

**What to avoid:** Do not encourage giving money, tolerating humiliation, or doing others’ homework to buy friendship.

*Editorial organization: ages are navigation aids, not developmental tests. See the linked entries for supporting guidance.*

### A10 · 10 years · Begin planning and learn about body changes

Breaking tasks down, puberty, and media judgment

**What children can practice**

- I can divide a large task into several sessions.
- I can ask about body changes without being ashamed of curiosity.

**What parents can do**

- Look at the real combined time needed for homework, activities, and rest.
- Discuss body changes and hygiene calmly and provide privacy.
- Before device use, agree on content, spending, and how to get help.

**When this happens:** An impossible schedule: check for overload and reduce adult-imposed demands too. Embarrassment about body changes: respond privately and do not discuss development in front of relatives.

**Try this today:** Remove or reduce one unclear-benefit commitment that is crowding out rest.

**What to look for:** Look for realistic plans and greater ease in asking questions.

**What to avoid:** Do not blame all overload on inefficiency or postpone body education until something goes wrong.

### A11 · 11 years · Have privacy and negotiate rules

Privacy, self-acceptance, and discussion

**What children can practice**

- I can disagree respectfully and explain why.
- I can have personal space and still seek help.

**What parents can do**

- Knock before entering and avoid sharing embarrassing stories or photos without permission.
- Explain supervision in advance and give reasons for necessary safety intervention.
- Comment less on appearance and pay more attention to physical feelings and daily functioning.

**When this happens:** Closing the bedroom door: invite conversation at a relaxed time and accept a temporary wish not to talk while staying connected. Appearance worries: listen and check for peer harassment; seek help for eating changes or other concerning signs.

**Try this today:** Let your child choose one family rule to discuss and explain a proposed change.

**What to look for:** Look for the ability to give reasons, not who argues more loudly.

**What to avoid:** Privacy does not mean no supervision; supervision does not mean unlimited access.

### A12 · 12 years · Learn new ways to get support in a new setting

School transitions, study methods, and recognizing pressure

**What children can practice**

- I can ask a teacher about the exact step I do not understand.
- I can acknowledge that a plan is not working and needs changing.

**What parents can do**

- Learn about classes, teachers, and support channels together.
- Notice eating, sleep, friendships, interests, and mood as well as marks.
- Use a brief regular check-in instead of questioning all day.

**When this happens:** Falling grades: examine foundations, changing demands, strategies, and wellbeing; address one important factor first. Arguing: allow disagreement while setting a mutual rule against insults, and return to the discussion when calm.

**Try this today:** Identify one school contact and one question your child is willing to ask.

**What to look for:** Notice earlier disclosure of difficulties and access to usable support.

**What to avoid:** Do not treat one change in results as proof of laziness or future failure.

### A13 · 13 years · Give independence a reliable base

Identity, emotions, and peer pressure

**What children can practice**

- I can make safe choices about interests and appearance.
- I can refuse a risky invitation and contact an adult to leave.

**What parents can do**

- Ask what interests mean to your child instead of mocking them.
- Agree on destination, companions, contact arrangements, and return travel.
- When help is requested, get them somewhere safe before reviewing what happened.

**When this happens:** “You do not understand”: ask what was missed instead of immediately defending yourself. Pressure to take risks: rehearse refusal, leaving, and contacting an adult; safety matters more than saving face.

**Try this today:** Agree on a short message your child can use when they need to be collected.

**What to look for:** Notice whether your child can contact you after a mistake or when frightened.

**What to avoid:** Never refuse help with “I told you so,” or label every request for independence as rebellion.

### A14 · 14 years · Recognize healthy relationships and digital boundaries

Consent, respect, media, and privacy

**What children can practice**

- I can like someone and still refuse contact or requests I do not want.
- I must not control others through passwords, threats, or sharing private information.

**What parents can do**

- Discuss relationships calmly without publicly interrogating private messages.
- Explain consent, coercion, and getting help before a crisis arises.
- Check whether night-time device use is displacing sleep and adjust together.

**When this happens:** Discovering a relationship: first consider respect, safety, power differences, and threats, then discuss daily arrangements. Requests for intimate images: stop sending and seek help; if something was already sent, do not blame the child—help prevent further harm.

**Try this today:** Discuss: “If someone threatens me after I say no, whom can I contact?”

**What to look for:** Look for recognition of respect and control, rather than judging solely by whether the child is dating.

**What to avoid:** Do not humiliate, expose private information, or turn safety education into fear-based lectures.

### A15 · 15 years · Exams matter, and so does wellbeing

Pressure, achievable goals, recovery, and support

**What children can practice**

- I can explain my worries and ask for useful help.
- One result does not define my worth.

**What parents can do**

- Turn goals into adjustable actions instead of repeatedly announcing success or failure.
- Protect sleep, activity, and conversations unrelated to exams.
- Investigate marked school avoidance or persistent low mood.

**When this happens:** “If I fail, everything is over”: recognize the pressure and discuss the exact fear and alternatives. Not attending school: investigate physical health, bullying, learning, and emotional factors rather than relying only on force or indefinite avoidance.

**Try this today:** Ask for one unhelpful reminder you could stop and one useful form of support you could provide.

**What to look for:** Look at functioning and distress; do not demand immediate cheerfulness.

**What to avoid:** Do not make basic care, food, or sleep conditional on results.

### A16 · 16 years · Practice autonomy through real responsibilities

Risk judgment, budgeting, practical tasks, and accountability

**What children can practice**

- I can manage some time and money and review the consequences.
- When something is unsafe, I seek help before debating blame.

**What parents can do**

- Teach cooking, travel planning, information checking, and other everyday skills.
- Test important choices on a small scale rather than relying only on imagination.
- Give outing rules reasons and review conditions instead of indefinite penalties.

**When this happens:** Wanting to stop a long-term activity: distinguish overload, setbacks, and a poor fit, then discuss a review period. Returning late without contact: confirm safety first, investigate the communication failure, and adjust future arrangements.

**Try this today:** Let your child plan a low-risk outing, then check safety and a backup plan together.

**What to look for:** Look for understanding of risks, costs, and support routes.

**What to avoid:** It is unreasonable to deny practice opportunities and then expect instant maturity.

### A17 · 17 years · Use information and experience to make choices

Education and career exploration, practical administration, and financial limits

**What children can practice**

- I can compare options, verify information, and acknowledge uncertainty.
- I can contact organizations and seek qualified advice.

**What parents can do**

- Explain available family resources and limits clearly.
- Investigate courses, costs, and actual work rather than reputation alone.
- Let your child manage materials and deadlines, with necessary adult review.

**When this happens:** Different views on a course or major: list reasons and check them against curricula and real experiences. Feeling directionless after a setback: restore everyday routines first, identify one near-term step, and seek help for serious distress.

**Try this today:** Create a comparison of interests, courses, costs, risks, and alternative routes.

**What to look for:** Look for choices supported by reasons; one decision need not settle an entire lifetime.

**What to avoid:** Do not make your child responsible for fulfilling your unrealized ambitions.

### A18 · 18 years · Continue support through adult cooperation

Autonomy, shared living, finances, and help-seeking

**What children can practice**

- I can take responsibility for decisions and still ask for reasonable support.
- Shared living requires discussion of chores, costs, and boundaries.

**What parents can do**

- Replace commands with agreements about the scope and duration of support.
- Respect correspondence and relationships; financial support must not become total control.
- Teach recovery and help-seeking instead of withdrawing all support abruptly at adulthood.

**When this happens:** Different lifestyles: separate personal preferences from effects on shared living. Returning for help after a poor decision: address safety and urgent losses, then involve the young adult in manageable repair.

**Try this today:** Agree on one responsibility to transfer gradually and the support still available.

**What to look for:** Notice autonomy and accountability developing together, rather than obedience alone.

**What to avoid:** Adulthood does not mean knowing everything, nor does it require parents to absorb every consequence.

### P01 · Pause your own anger before addressing the problem

1–18 years · Growing as a parent · Build the foundations · Professional guidance + practical suggestions

**Time and effort:** No purchase needed; adults need to practice pausing and arranging safe handovers.

**Intended improvement:** Return the conflict to a state where discussion is possible, rather than demand immediate obedience.

**What children can practice**

- I can tell an adult, “Your shouting frightens me.”
- I am not responsible for calming adults. If I fear being hurt, I can seek safety and a trusted adult.

**What parents can do**

- When your voice rises or you feel like hitting, stop the lecture and ensure the child is safely supervised.

**You could say:** “We want to understand the support you need, not give you a score.”

**What to avoid:** Do not dismiss concerns with “late talkers are gifted,” or casually attach diagnostic or intelligence labels.

**Observe and adjust:** Use professional advice for individual goals rather than trying to catch up in every area at once.

**When to seek further help:** Seek timely medical assessment for lost established skills, sudden change, or associated physical symptoms.

**Evidence and limits:** The source supports early action on concerns; age milestones are not diagnoses.

[S15 CDC: Concerned About Your Child’s Development?](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/families/concerned.html)

<a id="H05"></a>

### H05 · Protect sleep before discussing productivity

1–18 years · Difficulties and getting help · Build the foundations · Professional guidance + practical suggestions

**Time and effort:** Adjust schedules; products are usually unnecessary.

**Intended improvement:** Preserve a basic physical foundation for learning and emotional regulation.

**What children can practice**

- I can tell adults when I am tired or sleeping poorly; staying up late is not proof of effort.
- I can help plan how to end activities before bed.

**What parents can do**

- General total sleep per 24 hours: ages 1–2, 11–14 hours; 3–5, 10–13; 6–12, 9–12; 13–18, 8–10. Young children’s totals include naps.
- Check whether work, travel, extracurricular activities, or devices displace sleep, and build sustainable routines.
- Investigate environment, emotions, and physical problems instead of blaming poor sleep solely on self-control.

**You could say:** “There is not enough time. Let us adjust the tasks rather than make up for it by sleeping less every day.”

**What to avoid:** Do not use sleep deprivation as punishment or give sleep medicines or supplements without appropriate advice.

**Observe and adjust:** Consider actual sleep and daytime functioning; individual and medical needs follow professional advice.

**When to seek further help:** Seek medical assessment for persistent insomnia, marked daytime sleepiness, or snoring with breathing abnormalities.

**Evidence and limits:** Sleep ranges come from professional consensus; household scheduling suggestions are editorial.

[S19 AASM: Recommended Amount of Sleep for Pediatric Populations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/)

Read next: [D01 Set device rules around content, timing, and daily-life effects](#D01) · [L03 Find the homework obstacle before demanding speed](#L03)

<a id="H06"></a>

### H06 · Make real room for movement, meals, and rest

1–18 years · Difficulties and getting help · Build the foundations · Professional guidance + practical suggestions

**Time and effort:** Use available family resources; expensive lessons or equipment are not required.

**Intended improvement:** Treat healthy routines as a foundation rather than what remains after schoolwork.

**What children can practice**

- I can choose enjoyable activities suited to my ability.
- My body should not be mocked or ranked against others.

**What parents can do**

- Provide safe movement appropriate to age and physical condition: play for young children and increasing choice for older ones.
- Offer regular, reasonably balanced meals without forced clean plates, body shaming, or extreme restriction.
- Model the habits and preserve feasible movement and rest even during busy school periods.

**You could say:** “Would you prefer a walk, a ball game, or something else today? Let us choose something manageable.”

**What to avoid:** Do not use exercise as punishment, equate body size with character, or require activity through injury.

**Observe and adjust:** Consider sustainability and experience, not only weight or step counts.

**When to seek further help:** Seek medical support for pain, exercise-related symptoms, marked eating changes, or other physical concerns.

**Evidence and limits:** The source supports nutrition, movement, sleep, and modeling; this is not an individualized nutrition or exercise prescription.

[S24 CDC: Healthy Routines for Parents and Caregivers](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/tips-parents-caregivers/index.html)

## For children

You can have feelings and boundaries while learning to care for yourself and respect others. You have the right to ask for help.

<a id="K01"></a>

### Ages 1–3 · An adult narrates and practices with me · Someone responds to me; I can learn gradually

- I use pointing, sounds, and movement to show my needs. Adults can help put them into words.
- I can try picking up, putting away, and dressing. Adults protect me from danger.
- When I cry, I need help, not ridicule.
- I may feel angry, but adults will stop me from hurting others.

**Practice together:** Today, an adult practices noticing one need, saying a short sentence, and waiting for a response. Do not ask a toddler to memorize rules.

Read next: [C01 Respond to a toddler’s interest instead of continually testing](#C01) · [C02 Help a distressed young child settle while keeping limits clear](#C02) · [C03 Stop hitting or biting, then teach another response](#C03)

<a id="K02"></a>

### Ages 4–6 · Read and act it out together · I can say what I need and say no

- To join a game, I can ask, “May I play too?”
- If I do not want a hug, I can say, “I would like to wave hello.”
- If I break something, I can ask an adult to help me repair it.
- If someone frightens me, I can find a trusted adult. If one person does not help, I can tell another.

**Practice together:** Take turns with a caregiver acting out borrowing a toy and declining to lend it. Then swap roles.

Read next: [C06 Distinguish personal belongings from shared resources](#C06) · [R04 Practice repairing harm instead of forcing a quick apology](#R04) · [D03 Teach body boundaries early; protection remains an adult duty](#D03)

<a id="K03"></a>

### Ages 7–9 · I can begin reading on my own · I can ask when I do not know and repair mistakes

- When a task is difficult, I can show which step I do not understand and ask for help.
- I can use a checklist instead of waiting to be shouted at.
- One rejection from a friend does not mean everyone dislikes me.
- If someone keeps hurting me, adults need to protect me. I do not have to win a fight.
- I can tell the truth and take part in appropriate repair.

**Practice together:** Write or draw three people you can ask for help, including people at home and at school.

Read next: [L03 Find the homework obstacle before demanding speed](#L03) · [R01 When bullying occurs, protect, document, and follow up](#R01) · [R03 Make room for the truth when a child hides something](#R03)

<a id="K04"></a>

### Ages 10–12 · Having opinions and practicing judgment · I can have privacy and discuss rules

- I can ask about body changes; they are not shameful.
- I can disagree and explain why without insults.
- Something online is not necessarily true because it sounds certain. I can check.
- I can break large tasks into smaller ones and change a plan.
- A limited budget does not make me worth less than anyone else. I can compare before choosing.

**Practice together:** Choose one family rule. Write what it protects, what makes it difficult, and your suggested change.

Read next: [D04 Agree on privacy and supervision instead of relying on secret searches](#D04) · [L04 Use checking to understand your learning method](#L04) · [I02 Use a small budget to practice choices, not to measure worth](#I02)

<a id="K05"></a>

### Ages 13–15 · Boundaries, friends, and pressure · I deserve respect, and so do other people

- Liking someone does not require giving them my password or accepting unwanted contact.
- If friends push me into risks, I can refuse, leave, and seek help.
- A grade describes one performance, not my worth as a person.
- If I keep feeling distressed, sleeping badly, or struggling to attend school, I can ask a trusted adult and professionals for help.
- Even if I have made a mistake or been threatened, I still deserve protection.

**Practice together:** Prepare a sentence for leaving an uncomfortable situation and identify an adult who can collect or help you.

Read next: [D06 Discuss attraction and intimacy with clear consent and respect](#D06) · [D05 Prepare a safe exit from outings and risky peer pressure](#D05) · [H02 Support and assess persistent low mood or anxiety](#H02)

<a id="K06"></a>

### Ages 16–18 · Real life and gradual independence · I make choices and prepare for consequences and alternatives

- Before choosing study or work, I check courses, costs, actual work, and alternative paths.
- I practice appointments, budgeting, travel, and managing documents, and ask when I do not know.
- Living together means negotiating chores and expenses, not having identical opinions.
- I can repair setbacks and accept help without having to prove independence.
- Other people’s consent and privacy matter as much as my own boundaries.

**Practice together:** Complete a low-risk everyday task. Then note what you did independently, where you got stuck, and what you will do next time.

### Parent reflection: choose one change, not a score

- Does the child know at least one adult who can genuinely help?
- Is there a clear boundary against hitting, humiliation, and threats of abandonment?
- Before making demands, have adults taught the method and checked whether the task fits the child’s development?
- Does the child have meaningful choices suited to their abilities, rather than only obedience?
- Is there real room for sleep, food, movement, and rest?
- Do parents respect privacy and explain necessary safety supervision?
- After mistakes, is there a chance to repair rather than a permanent label?
- Do adults share tasks, get rest, and have their own routes to support?
- Does home–school communication record facts and actions, rather than only criticism?
- Are persistent difficulties receiving support rather than escalating punishment?
